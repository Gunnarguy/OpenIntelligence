OpenIntelligence. Pass one. The story, told as simply as I can tell it.

This is the first of five passes. Each pass tells the same story again, one level deeper. This one uses no technical words at all. If a technical word appears, I will say what it means the moment it appears. You can stop after this pass and you will have the whole picture, just drawn in crayon.

Here is the story.

Imagine a library. Not a public library. A private one, inside your phone, that only you can enter. Nobody else has a key. Nothing you put in it ever leaves the building unless you personally carry it out the door. That is the first and most important thing about this app. Everything happens inside the building.

The library has a loading dock, a reading room, a card catalog, a map on the wall, a desk, a writer, and a fact-checker. Let me walk you through what each one does, in the order things happen.

Part one. Putting a document into the library.

You hand a file to the loading dock. It might be a P D F, a Word document, a spreadsheet, a photo of a page, or a voice recording. The dock does not read it yet. It writes a ticket for it, puts the ticket in a queue, and works on one ticket at a time. Why a ticket? Because your phone is allowed to interrupt the library whenever it likes. If you switch apps or the battery dies halfway through, the ticket remembers exactly where the work stopped, and it picks up from there next time. Nothing gets done twice, and nothing gets lost.

The ticket goes to the reading room. Here the library has to get the words out of the file. If the file already contains real text, the reader copies it. If the file is a photo of a page, the reader has to look at the picture and read it the way you would read a sign across the street. If the page has a table, the reader is careful to keep the rows and columns lined up, because the number forty in the price column means something different from the number forty in the quantity column. If the file is a recording, a listener writes down what was said. All of this happens inside the building. The library never sends your page to anyone to be read.

Now the library has a long stretch of text. It cannot file the whole document as one thing, because later it will need to find one small fact inside it. So it cuts the text into index cards. Each card holds a paragraph or two. The cards overlap a little at the edges, so a sentence that lands right on a cut is not sliced in half and lost. And the library never cuts a table in half, for the reason we just talked about.

Here is the one genuinely strange idea in the whole app, and it is worth slowing down for.

On the wall of the library there is a map. Not a map of places. A map of meanings. Two things sit close together on this map if they mean similar things. The word dog and the word puppy are neighbours. The word dog and the phrase tax return are on opposite sides of the map. Every index card gets a pin on this map, placed according to what the card is about. Later, when you ask a question, your question gets a pin too, and the library simply looks at which cards are pinned nearby. That is how it can find a card that says S A E zero W twenty synthetic when you asked what oil your car takes, even though those two phrases share no words at all. They are neighbours on the map of meaning.

So each card is filed twice. Once in the card catalog, which is an ordinary index of exact words. And once on the map of meaning, as a pin. Two filing systems for the same card. Keep that in mind, because the whole rest of the story depends on it.

When every card is filed both ways, the ticket is stamped complete, and your document is in the library.

Part two. Asking a question.

You walk up to the desk and ask a question. Before anyone goes looking for anything, the librarian reads your question carefully and decides what kind of question it is. Are you looking up one fact? Do you want a summary of a whole book? Are you comparing two things? Is this a quick question or a hard one? The librarian also decides how hard to try. You can ask for a quick answer, a careful answer, or a maximum effort answer, and that choice changes how much of the library gets searched.

Then the search begins, and it begins in two places at once.

One helper goes to the card catalog and looks for cards containing the exact words you used. This helper is very good at names, part numbers, dates, and rare words.

At the same time, another helper puts a pin for your question on the map of meaning and gathers the cards pinned nearby. This helper is very good at understanding what you meant, even when you used different words.

The two helpers come back with two piles. The librarian shuffles them together into one ranked pile, giving credit to a card for being near the top of either helper's pile. A card that both helpers found rises to the top.

Now a second, slower reader looks at the top of the pile. This reader takes your question and each card together, side by side, and decides how well that specific card answers that specific question. This reader is too slow to look at the whole library, but on a short pile it is much smarter than either helper. It reorders the pile.

Then the librarian does two small tidying jobs. Cards that clearly do not answer the question are set aside. And if five cards at the top all say almost the same thing from the same page, some are swapped for cards from other books, so the answer is not lopsided. Sometimes the librarian also pulls the card just before and just after a good one, because a fact often continues onto the next card.

Part three. Writing the answer.

The best cards go onto the desk. The desk is small. It only fits so many cards, and that limit is real and fixed. So the librarian arranges the strongest cards first and last, because the writer, like all of us, pays less attention to the middle of a pile.

Now a decision. Usually the writer sits at this desk, inside the building, and writes the answer right here. But sometimes the pile is too big for this desk, or the question is genuinely hard. The library has an arrangement with one outside reading room, run by Apple, that is built so that not even Apple can look at what is read there. If the librarian thinks that room is needed, it stops, shows you exactly which cards would be carried over, and asks your permission. If you say no, the writer works at the small desk and does the best it can. Nothing ever leaves without you seeing it and saying yes.

The writer reads the cards on the desk and writes the answer. Every sentence of the answer has to point back at a card. Not a book, a card. Not a page, a line on a card.

Then the fact-checker arrives. The fact-checker does not trust the writer at all. It goes through the answer sentence by sentence and asks: is this sentence actually on one of the cards? Does this number match the card? Does this sentence contradict another card? Is the answer even about the same subject as the cards? Anything the fact-checker cannot find on a card gets struck out. If too much gets struck out, the library tells you honestly that it does not have enough in the building to answer, rather than making something up.

What you get back is the answer, with a little sticky note on every claim that jumps you to the exact line on the exact card it came from. And a small label that tells you where the writer sat: at the desk inside, or in the outside room you approved.

Part four. Why the library is built this way.

Every piece of this exists because of one promise and one danger.

The promise is that your documents stay yours. That is why there is a building with one key, why the reading and filing all happen inside, and why the one outside room is used only with your permission and only after you have seen what would go.

The danger is that a writer can invent things. A writer that sounds confident and is wrong is worse than no writer. That is why there are two filing systems, so the right cards are found more often. That is why there is a second slow reader. That is why every sentence must point at a card. And that is why the fact-checker exists and has the final say.

That is the whole story. A loading dock with tickets, a reading room, index cards, a card catalog and a map of meaning, two helpers searching at once, a slow careful reader, a small desk, a writer who must cite cards, a fact-checker with the last word, and one outside room you must approve.

In pass two we walk the same route again, and this time I will tell you the real names of each of these things and why each one was built the way it was.

OpenIntelligence. Pass two. The beginner's tour.

Same route as pass one. This time I will give you the real name of each part, say what it actually is, and say why it was built. I will still avoid numbers except the few worth remembering. When I use a technical word, I will define it in the same breath.

Let me start with the shape of the whole thing, because it helps to know there are two halves.

The first half is called ingestion. That is everything that happens when a document goes in: reading it, cutting it up, filing it. Ingestion happens once per document, and it is slow, and that is fine, because it only happens once.

The second half is called the query pipeline. That is everything that happens when you ask a question: understanding it, searching, choosing evidence, writing, checking. This has to be fast, because you are standing there waiting.

The app as a whole is a kind of system called retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG. The name means exactly what it says. Retrieval first: go and find the relevant pieces of your own documents. Then generation: have a language model write an answer using only those pieces. The retrieval part is what makes the answer about your documents. The generation part is what makes it readable. Keep those two words apart in your head, because almost every design decision in the app comes from keeping them apart.

One more idea before we walk. Some stages in this app are deterministic, meaning the same input always produces the same output, like a calculator. Cutting text, searching, ranking, checking citations, all deterministic. Other stages are generative, meaning a language model is asked to produce text, and it could produce something that is not true. There are only two generative stages in the whole app: rewriting your question, and writing the answer. Everything else is a calculator. That matters because a calculator cannot hallucinate. Only the two generative stages can, and so the checking is aimed at them.

Now the tour.

Ingestion, step one. The queue.

When you add a file, it becomes an ingestion item in a queue. The queue is durable, which means it is saved to disk with the item's stage and progress. If the app is killed, the item is restored on the next launch and continues from the last stage it finished. Items go through stages with names you will see on screen: queued, loading, extracting, chunking, analyzing, embedding, indexing, storing, complete. The queue processes one document at a time. Inside a single document there is plenty of parallel work, but the documents themselves go one after another. Two rules protect you here. If you delete a document, that deletion wins over any half-finished import that might try to bring it back. And if you add the same file twice, the app notices by its content and does not index it twice.

Ingestion, step two. Extraction.

This is getting the words out. The app picks a method by file type. For a P D F with a real text layer, it copies the text directly. But it does not trust the text layer blindly, because scanned P D Fs often carry a garbage text layer that would poison the index quietly. So it first samples a page, looks at it as an image, reads it, and compares. If the text layer is nonsense, every page goes through O C R instead.

O C R stands for optical character recognition, which is the phone looking at a picture and reading the letters. The app renders each page as a high-resolution image and hands it to Apple's Vision framework with the accurate setting and language correction turned on. It tells Vision which languages the document is in, because loading recognisers for thirteen languages on every page used to be a big waste. A separate pass reads tables as rows and columns so that a number keeps its column heading.

Office files are unzipped and their internal X M L is read directly. Spreadsheets and C S V files are parsed as tables. Audio and video are transcribed by Apple's speech recogniser, with on-device recognition required, so the audio never leaves the phone. Every one of these runs on the device.

Ingestion, step three. Chunking.

Chunking is cutting the text into pieces small enough to file and find. A chunk is at most three hundred and ten words. That number is not arbitrary. The model that reads chunks in the next step can only see about five hundred tokens at a time, and a token is roughly two thirds of a word, so three hundred and ten words with a little headroom fits. Chunks overlap by up to fifty words so nothing on a boundary is lost. Tables and lists are kept whole. And each chunk gets a short prefix naming the document and section it came from, because a chunk read on its own has lost what it is about, and that measurably hurts search.

There is one more check here that exists because of a real disaster. Words are not tokens, and the model does not complain when a chunk is too long. It silently keeps the first five hundred tokens and throws the rest away. Once, a bug made the token counter always return the same number, and more than half of every document was silently thrown away while every log said everything was fine. So now the app runs every chunk through the real tokenizer before embedding it and splits anything that is too long.

Ingestion, step four. Embedding.

Embedding is the map of meaning from pass one. An embedding model reads a chunk and produces a list of three hundred and eighty-four numbers. Those numbers are the chunk's coordinates on the map. Chunks about similar things get similar coordinates. The model is a small one that runs on the device. The list is scaled so that comparing two chunks is a single multiplication, which matters later for speed.

Why three hundred and eighty-four? It is the width this particular model produces, and it is small enough that a whole library's worth of coordinates can stay in a file that the phone can read without loading it all into memory.

Ingestion, step five. Indexing.

Now each chunk is filed twice.

The first filing system is a full-text search index. It is built on SQLite, which is a small database engine inside the phone, using a feature called F T S five, which is a fast index of exact words. The ranking formula it uses is called B M twenty-five. You do not need to know the formula. You need to know what it is good at: exact words, part numbers, names, codes. And you need to know that words in a section title count more than the same word buried in a paragraph, because the app told the index to weight them that way.

The second filing system is the vector store, which is where the coordinates live. The word vector just means the list of numbers. The vectors for a library are written to one flat file and read by memory mapping, which means the phone reads the file directly as if it were memory, without copying it. That is how a library of tens of thousands of chunks costs almost no memory to search.

On top of the two indexes, the app derives a few extra things: a one-paragraph summary of each document, so a question like summarise this can be answered from summaries rather than from forty similar chunks; and a list of names and entities, so a question about a specific name can be expanded to every chunk that mentions it.

When both indexes are written and saved, the item is complete.

That is ingestion. Now the query pipeline.

Query, step one. Understanding the question.

Before anything is searched, the app profiles your question. What kind of answer do you want: a lookup, a summary, a comparison, a procedure? How complex is it? Which quality mode did you pick? The three modes are Standard, Deep Think, and Maximum. Standard is one pass through the pipeline. Deep Think and Maximum are agentic, a word I will explain in a moment. Out of this profiling comes a plan for the query, and a decision about whether it will run as a single pass or as a loop.

The app may also rewrite your question. Users type short vague questions and the index was built from long specific text, so a model can be asked to expand the question, or to write a short imaginary answer whose coordinates are then used for the search. That trick has a name, hypothetical document embedding, and it exists because a paragraph lands closer to other paragraphs on the map than a question does.

Query, step two. Search, twice, at the same time.

The question's coordinates are computed with the same embedding model. Then two searches run in parallel. One is the vector search: find the chunks whose coordinates are nearest. The other is the keyword search through F T S five. Each returns a ranked list of candidates, more than you will ultimately need.

The two lists are merged with a method called reciprocal rank fusion. The idea is simple. A chunk gets points for being near the top of either list, and more points the higher it is. A chunk near the top of both lists ends up first. The merge only uses positions, not scores, which is the whole point: the two searches produce scores that mean completely different things, and positions are the only thing they have in common.

Query, step three. Rerank.

Now the second, slower reader from pass one. It is called a cross-encoder. The first search compared the question's coordinates to each chunk's coordinates separately, which is fast but shallow. The cross-encoder reads the question and one chunk together, as a pair, and judges how well that chunk answers that question. It is far more accurate and far slower, so it only runs on the short list from the merge, never on the whole library. The list is reordered by its judgement.

Query, step four. Filtering and spreading.

Chunks below a similarity floor are dropped. The floor is lower in Deep Think and lower again in Maximum, because those modes prefer to gather more and let later steps sort it out. Then a step called maximal marginal relevance, or M M R, spreads the results: it penalises a chunk for being nearly identical to one already chosen, so five copies of the same paragraph do not crowd out a second document. Then the app pulls neighbouring chunks, and the parent section when it helps, because a fact often continues across a cut.

Query, step five. Packing the desk.

The language model on the device has a fixed window of about four thousand tokens, and every token spent on evidence is one not available for the answer. So there is a budget. The app subtracts the instructions, the question, a safety margin and the space reserved for the answer, and fills what is left with the best chunks, strongest first and last. Chunks that do not fit are set aside and recorded, so the diagnostics can show you what was left out.

Query, step six. Deciding where to write.

Only now, after retrieval, does the app decide which model writes the answer. It builds an execution plan with four possible outcomes. Abstain, if there is not enough evidence. Deterministic, if a rule can extract the answer directly, say a single value from a table. On device, which is the normal case. Or Private Cloud Compute, which is Apple's outside room. The outside room is chosen only when the evidence does not fit the local window or the question is complex enough to need it, and only if the network is up and you have consented. Before it asks you, it builds the exact trimmed set of chunks that would be sent and shows you that. If you decline, it writes on the device. Every cloud plan has an on-device fallback.

Query, step seven. Generation.

The model is Apple's on-device language model, reached through a framework called Foundation Models. The app opens a session, gives it instructions, gives it the packed evidence and your question, and streams the answer back word by word so you see it as it forms. The answer is not free text. It is a structured answer: a list of claims, each with the identifiers of the chunks that support it. That structure is what makes the next step possible.

Query, step eight. Verification.

Nine checks, called gates, lettered A to I, run in order. They are all deterministic. Gate A asks whether retrieval was confident enough at all. Gate B asks whether every claim cites evidence. Gate C checks that numbers in the answer appear in the sources. Gate D looks for contradictions between sources. Gate E, the strongest one, puts the answer itself on the map of meaning and checks that it lands near its own evidence; an answer far from its evidence is treated as invented. F checks quotes, G checks the writing, H checks the answer is complete, and I, applied earlier, keeps evidence from unrelated domains out. Claims that fail are removed or marked unsupported. If too little survives, the app says it does not have enough evidence.

Query, step nine. What you get.

A typed answer with citations that jump to the exact character range in the source. A label saying which model actually completed the answer, on device or cloud, based on what ran rather than what was requested. A diagnostics view showing how many chunks were found, kept, dropped and packed. And a trace log on disk recording every stage with timings, for when something goes wrong.

Now the word agentic.

In Standard mode, the pipeline runs once. In Deep Think and Maximum, an orchestrator takes over. It runs the pipeline, reads what came back, decides whether it has enough, and if not, rewrites the question or breaks it into sub-questions and searches again. It carries a notebook of facts between rounds, because the model's window is small and a fresh session is opened for each round. It stops when it is confident enough, when new searches stop finding anything new, when a time budget runs out, or when it hits a hard cap on rounds. All of the thinking happens on the device. Only the final write-up may go to the outside room, through the same plan and consent as before.

Last thing for this pass. The hardware.

Your phone has three kinds of processor: the C P U, the G P U, and the Neural Engine. People love to say which one runs what, and most of what gets said is not something the app can know. Here is what is actually true. The app can explicitly use the G P U for one thing: comparing your question's coordinates against a very large library, using a small program called a Metal kernel. It does that when the library has a thousand or more chunks. For the embedding model and the reranker, the app can only ask Apple's Core M L framework which processors it is allowed to use, and Core M L decides. For O C R, speech, and the language model itself, the app has no say at all; Apple's frameworks decide and do not report back. So when someone asks where the Neural Engine runs, the honest answer is: the app requests it in a few places, and Apple decides.

That is the beginner's tour. You now know the real names: ingestion and query, deterministic and generative, chunk, embedding, vector, F T S five and B M twenty-five, reciprocal rank fusion, cross-encoder, M M R, token budget, execution plan, Private Cloud Compute, Foundation Models, structured answer, the nine gates, and agentic.

OpenIntelligence. Pass three. An engineer walks a newcomer through it.

You know the route now. This pass adds the numbers, the design decisions, where each stage runs, and what each stage quietly throws away. I am going to talk the way I would talk to a good engineer on their first week: assuming you can follow, but not assuming you know this codebase. Every number I say here was read from the source and is written down with its line in the full system trace document, so you can check any of them.

Section one. What happens when the app launches.

Less than you would think. The app struct wires two bridges so background work can talk to the ingestion and query services, configures tips, and on i O S registers five background task identifiers with the system: continued ingestion, continued query, index maintenance, Spotlight reindex, and app refresh. Then the root view constructs the service graph in one place, in order: workspace sync, the container service, StoreKit billing, the entitlement store, the RAG service, settings, model resolution, onboarding state, and what's-new state. The RAG service in turn builds the document processor, the embedding service, the vector store router, the context packer, and the extractive summariser.

Here is what does not happen at launch. The embedding model is not loaded; only its tokenizer is, because token counting is needed before any embedding. The full-text database is not opened; it opens on first use. The only model load at launch is the reranker, and that runs in a detached task with all compute units permitted. So the first query pays the cost of compiling the embedding model and opening the database, not the launch.

Two timers matter. Workspace changes are debounced two seconds before a sync pass. And there is a known defect: a timer in the workspace sync service fires every one point six eight seconds while the app is idle, because a container's orphaned state never resolves. It used to reload every vector store on every tick, which produced nearly three thousand loads in under three minutes of sitting still. The router now compares an on-disk signature first, so the tick is cheap. The tick itself is still there. That is the open item in the state file.

Section two. Ingestion, with numbers.

The queue is serial. One document at a time, always. Parallelism is inside a document: pages are extracted in a task group bounded by the device's Vision concurrency, and embeddings run in a task group bounded by the device's embedding concurrency. Both bounds come from a device capability ladder I will describe in section eight.

If the file is in iCloud and is only a placeholder, the app waits up to twenty seconds for it to download before reading. Files over five hundred megabytes that cannot be streamed are rejected outright, because loading them into a string would take up to two gigabytes.

Extraction for a P D F starts with text-layer validation. Take the page with the most native text, at least fifty characters. Render it. Run quick O C R on the render. If the native text is garbled compared with what Vision reads, route every page through O C R. Pages are rendered at three hundred and sixty D P I, which is the point size times five. On i O S that goes through U I Graphics Image Renderer with an opaque format; on mac O S it goes straight into a bitmap context, because the old path through N S Image produced images four times larger than requested and three hundred and seventy megabytes per page. Rendering is C P U work; it is not the bottleneck. On a real trace, render is twelve to sixteen milliseconds a page. A five-hour ingest is Vision recognition time, not rasterisation.

O C R is V N Recognize Text Request, accurate level, language correction on, and the recognition languages narrowed to the ones detected for this document. The structure pass is a different request, Recognize Documents Request, which reads rows and columns, and it runs at half resolution unless the page is flagged high risk. A complexity analyser decides that by rendering at one hundred and forty-four D P I and looking for grid lines and figures. Vision calls are throttled through an actor-based semaphore sized to the device, with a short cooldown between operations so G P U command buffers settle.

Audio is transcribed by S F Speech Recognizer with on-device recognition required, in segments of at most ten minutes. There is a newer Speech Analyzer code path in the source. It never compiles. It is behind a can-import check for a module that does not exist, and it calls an A P I shape the S D K does not declare. Every build takes the legacy path. That is a finding from this week, and it is the kind of thing you should expect to find in a codebase this size: a branch that looks live and is dead.

Chunking. The hard ceiling is three hundred and ten words. The largest target is two hundred and sixty. Overlap is capped at fifty. The smallest target is one hundred. Then the tokenizer check: the model accepts five hundred and ten tokens; eighty are reserved for the contextual prefix; so every chunk is validated at four hundred and thirty tokens using the real tokenizer, the same Rust-backed one the embedding provider uses. A chunk still oversized after splitting is logged and will be truncated by the model. Note the shape of that failure: it is logged, it is not counted anywhere a user sees.

Embedding. The default provider is Core M L with a Mini L M model producing three hundred and eighty-four dimensions. On i O S twenty-seven and mac O S twenty-seven, a Core A I provider becomes the default, loading the same model through Apple's newer compilation path; saved Core M L defaults are migrated to it automatically. Same vector space, same width. One inference is: tokenise, fill three pre-allocated input arrays of length five hundred and twelve, run prediction, mean-pool the token states under the attention mask, and L two normalise. Batches of more than four texts run in a task group with width equal to the device's embedding concurrency.

Which processor? The Core M L path sets compute units from the user's G P U execution profile. Efficiency and Balanced request C P U plus Neural Engine, deliberately excluding the G P U because the Neural Engine is the efficient unit for this. Performance and Maximum request all units. Core M L then places each layer. Until late August, Maximum requested C P U plus G P U, which excluded the Neural Engine and made the top profile slower than the one below it. The Core A I path exposes no placement control at all.

Persistence. Vectors go to a container's vector store: store batch, then persist, which writes the vectors file, the norms file and a metadata file atomically and re-maps the vectors. Then Spotlight, if enabled. Then the chunk rows go to F T S five. Then vocabulary and entity learning, then the optional document summary, one model call, then a second persist, then content tags. The document-level full text went to F T S five much earlier, inside extraction, before chunking. So there is a window where a document is lexically searchable at document level and absent from both chunk indexes. There is no transaction across the two stores. The restore path is what reconciles a crash in that window.

The full-text database has nine tables. Four are F T S five virtual tables: documents, chunks, document pages, and a vocabulary table. Five are ordinary tables for structured elements, table rows, document content, document metadata, and the semantic query cache. The chunks table indexes section title, section path, and content, tokenised with Porter stemming, and leaves the identifiers, page number and structure type unindexed. It uses write-ahead logging, and a busy timeout of three seconds so ingestion and a query touching the database together wait instead of failing.

Section three. The query, with numbers. Standard mode.

The chat screen builds an inference config from the settings and calls the RAG service's query method, which binds a stream handler and calls the internal query. The first thing the internal query does is ask the query runtime coordinator to resolve everything: quality mode, P C C eligibility, adaptive configuration, the query profile, and whether this run is agentic. If it is agentic, we leave for section five. Otherwise:

Step zero, the corpus vocabulary is fetched. Step one, optional model-driven query understanding if rewriting is enabled, then corpus-aware expansion and intent classification. Step two, the query is embedded with the same provider; a semantic cache hit skips this. Step three, hybrid search.

Hybrid search runs the vector search and the F T S five search concurrently. With the Standard initial top K of thirty, the vector side asks for ninety candidates, three times K, and the F T S side asks for the smaller of three times K and sixty. Structured rows get up to thirty-six. Fusion is reciprocal rank fusion with k equal to sixty, vector weight zero point seven, keyword weight zero point three, done off the main thread on a snapshot of the candidates so the document-frequency statistics are valid. After fusion, keyword-only hits that fell below the cut are re-attached, up to the larger of four and K over six, so an exact part-number match is never lost to paraphrase. Section title and path matches get a boost.

Now the vector search itself, because this is where the G P U question is answered honestly. The vector store's search method has a threshold of one thousand. If the container has at least one thousand vectors and a Metal device with a command queue exists, the memory-mapped buffer is handed to a Metal kernel with no copy; above a thousand documents a threadgroup kernel is used, otherwise a SIMD kernel. Below the threshold, or without a G P U, it is C P U Accelerate: one matrix multiply over the mapped buffer if the count exceeds the device's batch threshold, which is sixteen on M-series Macs, and one dot product per vector otherwise. Two things people get wrong: the user's G P U profile does not gate this path, and the G P U path requires a compiled kernel, which can fail.

Step four, rerank. The reranker is a Core M L cross-encoder loaded with all compute units and low-precision G P U accumulation allowed. Query and passage are tokenised together to five hundred and twelve tokens and scored concurrently in a task group. It is called with top K times three and trimmed to that. Step four point three, the similarity floor: zero point two eight in Standard, zero point two five in Deep Think, zero point two zero in Maximum. Lenient mode lowers it; procedural questions raise it. Step four point four ensures more than one document is represented. Step four point five, M M R with lambda zero point six zero, zero point five five, zero point five zero by mode; the pairwise similarity matrix goes to Metal when there are more than fifty candidates, the profile allows Metal vector operations, and a G P U exists. Then parent retrieval, cross-reference resolution, and a targeted specification search. Then optional contextual compression, followed by a one-second sleep to let the Foundation Models rate budget recover.

Step five, context assembly. The budget: a base window of four thousand and ninety-six tokens, a default evidence budget of three thousand two hundred, a safety reserve of two hundred and fifty-six, estimated at one point four characters per token on device. The real window is read from the system model at runtime and can be larger. Chunks that do not fit are trimmed and their identifiers recorded.

Section four. Where the answer runs.

Cosine similarity. Cos of q and d equals q dot d over the product of their L two norms. B M twenty-five. The thread.

Everything in this app comes from one promise and one danger.

The promise is that your documents stay yours. So: one private container per library, all reading and filing on the device, memory-mapped vectors so a phone can hold a library, and a single outside room, Apple's Private Cloud Compute, used only after retrieval, only after the app has built and shown you the exact payload, only with your consent, and always with an on-device fallback.

The danger is that a language model invents. So: two indexes instead of one, so the right evidence is found more often. A cross-encoder second opinion. A similarity floor and diversity so the desk is not filled with the wrong or the same thing. A hard token budget so nothing is silently dropped without being recorded. Only two generative stages, and nine deterministic gates aimed at them. Citations to character ranges, so every claim can be checked. And an honest label of which model actually completed the answer.

If you remember nothing else, remember that every part of the app is one of those two things: keeping the promise, or defending against the danger.
